\begin{abstract}
This thesis presents a finite element framework for the numerical simulation of parameterized incompressible Stokes flows together with a reduced-order modeling approach for efficient many-query computations. A stable mixed finite element discretization is employed to accurately resolve the coupled velocity and pressure fields while satisfying the incompressibility constraint. Based on the resulting full-order model, a reduced-order model is constructed using Proper Orthogonal Decomposition, enabling efficient approximation of the solution over varying parameter values. The proposed methodology is validated through numerical experiments, demonstrating that the reduced model achieves substantial computational savings while maintaining high accuracy with respect to the full-order simulations.

\section{Introduction to Stokes Equations and Finite Element Method}
Stokes flow, or \emph{creeping flow}, describes incompressible fluid motion in which viscous forces dominate inertial effects, characterized by a low Reynolds number,

\[
\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho U L}{\mu}\ll1,\qquad \nu=\frac{\mu}{\rho},
\]

where $\rho$ is the fluid density, $U$ a characteristic velocity, $L$ a characteristic length scale, $\mu$ the dynamic viscosity, and $\nu$ the kinematic viscosity. The regime arises whenever motion is slow, viscosity is high, or the length scale is very small, making it the governing model for microfluidics, MEMS, coating and polymer processing, biological transport, microorganism locomotion, particle settling, and slow mantle convection. First analyzed by Sir George Gabriel Stokes in his study of viscous settling, the equations remain today a foundational model in fluid mechanics and the basis for a wide range of numerical and reduced-order methods.
 
\subsection{Mathematical Characterization and Challenges}
The Stokes equations follow from linearizing the incompressible Navier--Stokes equations,
\[
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\dfrac{1}{\rho} \nabla p + \nu \nabla^2 \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{f}\\[0.8em]
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0 
\end{cases}
\]
where $\mathbf{u}$ is the velocity field, $t$ time, $p$ the pressure field, $\rho$ the constant density, $\nu$
the kinematic viscosity, and $\mathbf{f}$ external body forces. Dropping the convective term
$(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}$ linearizes the momentum equation, leaving $\mathbf{u}$ and $p$ coupled
purely through viscous dissipation and incompressibility.
 
This simplification nonetheless introduces two structural challenges for the Finite Element Method:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Saddle-Point Structure:} The constraint $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$ gives the weak
    formulation a \textit{saddle-point} (constrained-optimization) algebraic structure.
    \item \textbf{The Inf-Sup (LBB) Condition:} Velocity and pressure spaces $X_h, Q_h$ cannot be paired
    arbitrarily as in standard elliptic problems; unstable pairings produce spurious ``checkerboard''
    pressure oscillations. Stability requires the \textit{Ladyzhenskaya-Babu\v{s}ka-Brezzi (LBB)} condition,
    satisfied by pairings such as Taylor-Hood ($P_k/P_{k-1}$) or stabilized formulations.
\end{itemize}
 
\subsection{Stable Macro-Element Discretizations: The \texorpdfstring{$P_1\text{-iso-}P_1$}{P1-iso-P1} Element}
The $P_1\text{-iso-}P_1$ element, introduced by Bercovier and Pironneau, satisfies the LBB condition without
resorting to higher-order or non-conforming spaces. Rather than varying polynomial degree, it varies mesh
density: both velocity and pressure remain piecewise-linear ($P_1$), but velocity lives on a fine mesh
$\mathcal{T}_h$ obtained by uniformly refining a coarse macro-mesh $\mathcal{T}_H$ -- each macro-triangle
$K\in\mathcal T_H$ split into four congruent children by connecting edge midpoints, halving the mesh size to
$h=H/2$.
 
The discrete spaces are
\begin{align*}
X_h &= \{ \mathbf{v}_h \in C^0(\overline{\Omega})^2 : \mathbf{v}_h|_K \in P_1(K)^2, \forall K \in \mathcal{T}_h \} \\
Q_h &= \{ q_h \in C^0(\overline{\Omega}) : q_h|_M \in P_1(M), \forall M \in \mathcal{T}_H \}
\end{align*}
Placing $\mathbf u_h$ on the fine grid and $p_h$ on the coarse grid enriches velocity with far more internal
degrees of freedom than pressure, yielding a stable ratio that satisfies the discrete LBB condition,
\[
\exists \beta>0: \quad
\inf_{q \in Q_{\setminus\{0\}}} \space \sup_{v \in V_{\setminus\{0\}}} \left(\frac{\int_{\Omega}q(\nabla \cdot v)d\Omega}{\|v\|_{V}\cdot\|q\|_{Q}}\right) \ge \beta 
\]
with $\beta$ bounded away from zero independently of refinement. The result is a highly symmetric
macro-element that keeps the low cost of linear shape functions while retaining robust convergence.

\section{Continuous and Variational Formulations}
\subsection*{Strong Formulation}
The steady-state motion of an incompressible viscous fluid in the low-Reynolds-number regime is governed by the classical \textit{Stokes equations}. These equations constitute a fundamental linear model in fluid mechanics and describe the balance between viscous stresses, pressure forces, and the incompressibility constraint. Given a bounded domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ enclosed by a boundary $\partial\Omega = \Gamma_D \cup \Gamma_N$ (where $\Gamma_D \cap \Gamma_N = \emptyset$), the strong formulation of the problem seeks the velocity field $\mathbf{u}(x,y)$ and the scalar pressure field $p(x,y)$ satisfying:

\[
\begin{cases}
-\nu \Delta u + \nabla p = 0
& \text{in } \Omega, \\[0.5em]

\nabla \cdot u = 0
& \text{in } \Omega, \\[0.5em]

u = g
& \text{on } \Gamma_D, \\[0.5em]

-p\,n + \nu (\nabla u)n = h
& \text{on } \Gamma_N.
\end{cases}
\]

The mathematical and physical roles of these individual components are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Momentum Balance ($-\nu \Delta \mathbf{u} + \nabla p = \mathbf{0}$):} This represents the conservation of momentum. It dictates a strict equilibrium between the internal viscous shear stresses—scaled by the kinematic viscosity $\nu$—and the driving force generated by the pressure gradient $\nabla p$.
    \item \textbf{Incompressibility Constraint ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$):} This serves as the conservation of mass equation for an incompressible fluid. It requires the velocity field to be divergence-free, ensuring that fluid volume is perfectly preserved at every point within the domain.
    \item \textbf{Dirichlet Boundary Condition ($\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{g}$):} Enforced on the boundary segment $\Gamma_D$, this prescribes the exact fluid velocity. It is commonly used to dictate non-zero flow profiles at fluid inlets or to impose the physical ``no-slip'' condition ($\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}$) along stationary solid walls.
    \item \textbf{Neumann Boundary Condition ($-p\mathbf{n} + \nu (\nabla \mathbf{u})\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{h}$):} Enforced on the boundary segment $\Gamma_N$ with outward unit normal vector $\mathbf{n}$, this specifies the weak normal stress or traction force vector $\mathbf{h}$ acting on the fluid, typically representing free-pressure outlets or open boundaries.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Weak Formulation}

To solve the Stokes system using the Finite Element Method, we transform the original strong partial differential equations into their weak (or variational) integral form. This is achieved by multiplying the momentum and continuity equations by smooth test functions, $\mathbf{v}$ and $q$, and integrating the terms over the entire physical domain $\Omega$. Applying integration by parts (Green's identity) to the second-order viscous diffusion term reduces the continuity requirements on our velocity fields from second-order derivatives down to first-order derivatives. 

During this mathematical transition, the natural boundary conditions—such as the traction force $h$ on the Neumann boundary $\Gamma_N$—automatically emerge within the boundary integrals. To accommodate non-zero (inhomogeneous) Dirichlet velocity profiles on the walls while strictly maintaining a homogeneous test function space $X$ where $\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$ on $\Gamma_D$, we apply a boundary lifting technique. We decompose the true velocity field into $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}_h + \mathbf{r}_g$, where $\mathbf{r}_g$ is a known extension function matching the boundary data, and $\mathbf{u}_h \in X$ is the remaining homogeneous unknown field. This systematic process yields the coupled weak formulation system:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stokes_weak}
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle
\int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla u : \nabla v \, d\Omega
-
\int_{\Omega} p \, (\nabla \cdot v)\, d\Omega
=
\int_{\Gamma_N} h \cdot v \, d\Gamma
-
\int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla r_g : \nabla v \, d\Omega,
& \forall v \in X,
\\[1.2em]

\displaystyle
-\int_{\Omega} q \, (\nabla \cdot u)\, d\Omega
=
\int_{\Omega} q \, (\nabla \cdot r_g)\, d\Omega,
& \forall q \in Q.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\section{Finite Element Discretization and Matrix Assembly}

In this section, we transition from the continuous \textbf{weak formulation} to a \textbf{discrete algebraic representation} suitable for computational execution. By projecting the infinite-dimensional solution space onto a finite-dimensional subspace spanned by piecewise linear basis functions, we transform the governing integral operators into global sparse matrices. The following subsections detail the systematic assembly of these components.

\subsection{Reference Triangle}

To compute integrals efficiently, we map every arbitrary triangle in our mesh onto a standard master element called the reference triangle $\hat{T}$ in local coordinates $(\xi_1, \xi_2)$. Its vertices are fixed at $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_1 = (0,0)$, $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_2 = (1,0)$, and $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_3 = (0,1)$. On this master domain, we define the standard linear Lagrange shape functions $\hat{\phi}_i(\xi_1, \xi_2)$:


\begin{tcolorbox}[
    colframe=heaven, 
    colback=heaven!7!white, 
    sharp corners
]
\begin{center}
$
\begin{aligned}
\displaystyle
    \hat{\phi}_1(\xi_1, \xi_2) &= 1 - \xi_1 - \xi_2 &\hspace{113pt}
    & 
    \hat{\phi}_1(0, 0) = 1, \quad 
    \hat{\phi}_1(1, 0) = 0, \quad
    \hat{\phi}_1(0, 1) = 0
    \\[5pt]
    \hat{\phi}_2(\xi_1, \xi_2) &= \xi_1 &\hspace{113pt}
    &
    \hat{\phi}_2(0, 0) = 0, \quad 
    \hat{\phi}_2(1, 0) = 1, \quad
    \hat{\phi}_2(0, 1) = 0
    \\[5pt]
    \hat{\phi}_3(\xi_1, \xi_2) &= \xi_2 &\hspace{113pt}
    &
    \hat{\phi}_3(0, 0) = 0, \quad 
    \hat{\phi}_3(1, 0) = 0, \quad
    \hat{\phi}_3(0, 1) = 1
    \\[5pt]
\end{aligned}
$
\end{center}
\end{tcolorbox}

We will also take the gradients for easier future derivations and implementations:

\begin{tcolorbox}[
    colframe=heaven, 
    colback=heaven!7!white, 
    sharp corners
]
\begin{center}
$
\begin{array}{ccc}
\nabla \hat{\phi}_1(\xi_1,\xi_2)
=\begin{bmatrix}
    -1\\
    -1
\end{bmatrix}
&\qquad\qquad
\nabla \hat{\phi}_2(\xi_1,\xi_2)
=\begin{bmatrix}
    1\\
    0
\end{bmatrix}
&\qquad\qquad
\nabla \hat{\phi}_3(\xi_1,\xi_2)
=\begin{bmatrix}
    0\\
    1
\end{bmatrix}
\end{array}
$
\end{center}
\end{tcolorbox}

We will now come up with the proper mapping and check it:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\left.
\begin{array}{cc}
    x(\xi_1, \xi_2) &= x_1 + (x_2 - x_1)\xi_1 + (x_3 - x_1)\xi_2\\[5pt]
    y(\xi_1, \xi_2) &= y_1 + (y_2 - y_1)\xi_1 + (y_3 - y_1)\xi_2
\end{array}
\right|
\qquad 
\begin{array}{ccc}
    x(0, 0) = x_1 \quad & \quad x(1, 0) = x_2 \quad & \quad x(0, 1) = x_3\\[5pt]
    y(0, 0) = y_1 \quad & \quad y(1, 0) = y_2 \quad & \quad y(0, 1) = y_3
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
\]

\vspace{2em}
The Jacobian is derived as follows:

\[
\mathbf{J}_T = \begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial x}{\partial \xi_1} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial \xi_2} \\[9pt]
\frac{\partial y}{\partial \xi_1} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial \xi_2}
\end{bmatrix} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    x_2 - x_1 & x_3 - x_1 \\[13pt]
    y_2 - y_1 & y_3 - y_1
\end{bmatrix}
\]

\vspace{2em}
It is also necessary to take a look at the original shape functions' derivatives:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:grad_for_Bx_By}
\begin{aligned}
    \nabla \phi_i(x,y) &=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \frac{\partial \phi _i}{\partial x}\\[5pt]
        \frac{\partial \phi _i}{\partial y}
    \end{bmatrix}
    = \mathbf{J}_T^{-T} \cdot \nabla \hat{\phi}_i(\xi_1, \xi_2)
    = \left[
    \begin{array}{cc}
       x_2 - x_1 & x_3 - x_1 \\
       y_2 - y_1 & y_3 - y_1
    \end{array}
    \right]^{-T}
    \cdot
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi_1}\\[5pt]
        \frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi_2}
    \end{bmatrix}=\\[10pt]
    &=\left|\mathbf{X}=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        a & b\\
        c & d
    \end{bmatrix},\quad
    \mathbf{X}^{-1}=  \frac{1}{ad-cb}
    \begin{bmatrix}
         d & -b \\
        -c &  a
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right| 
    \\[10pt]
    &= \frac{1}{|\mathbf{J}_T|} 
    \begin{bmatrix}
         y_3 - y_1 & y_1 - y_2 \\
         x_1 - x_3 & x_2 - x_1 
    \end{bmatrix}\cdot \begin{bmatrix}
        \frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi_1}\\[5pt]
        \frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi_2}
    \end{bmatrix}=
    \\[10pt]
    &= |\mathbf{J}_T|^{-1}
        \begin{bmatrix}            
        \quad (y_3 - y_1)\frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi _1} - (y_2 - y_1)\frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi _2}\\[5pt]
        \space-(x_3 - x_1)\frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi _1} + (x_2 - x_1)\frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi _2}
        \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stiffness Matrix \texorpdfstring{$\mathbf{A}$}{A}}\label{sec:stiffness_matrix}

The stiffness matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is the discrete counterpart of the viscous diffusion term, and it is this operator that carries the physical viscosity $\nu$ into the algebraic system: every velocity degree of freedom is coupled to its neighbors through $\mathbf{A}$, in exactly the same way the continuous Laplacian couples a velocity field to itself through spatial derivatives. As with every operator derived in this chapter, its construction starts directly from the corresponding term in the weak formulation and proceeds by substituting the finite element expansion of the trial and test functions.

\subsubsection*{Weak formulation}
To determine the discrete operator for the viscous term, we begin with the bilinear form involving the velocity test and trial functions:

\begin{tcolorbox}[
    colframe=ForestGreen, 
    colback=ForestGreen!7!white, 
    sharp corners
]
\begin{center}
   $\displaystyle \int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla u : \nabla v \, d\Omega$ 
\end{center}
\end{tcolorbox}

\[
\begin{aligned}    
\displaystyle
\int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla \mathbf{u}(x,y) : \nabla \mathbf{v}(x,y)  \, d\Omega &= 
\left|\begin{array}{c}
   \mathbf{u}(x,y) =\begin{bmatrix}
       u_x(x,y) \\
       u_y(x,y) 
   \end{bmatrix}
   \\[17pt]
   \mathbf{v}(x,y) =\begin{bmatrix}
       v_x(x,y) \\
       v_y(x,y) 
   \end{bmatrix}
\end{array}\right|
=\int_{\Omega} \nu \left(\nabla u_x \cdot \nabla v_x + \nabla u_y \cdot \nabla v_y \right) \, d\Omega= \\[10pt]
&= \int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla u_x \cdot \nabla v_x \, d\Omega +\int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla u_y \cdot \nabla v_y \, d\Omega 
= \left|\begin{array}{c}
u_x(x,y) \approx \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^x_i\phi_i(x,y) \\[17pt]
u_y(x,y) \approx \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^y_i\phi_i(x,y) 
\end{array} \right|= \\[10pt]
&=\int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla \left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^x_i\phi_i \right) \cdot \nabla v_x \, d\Omega +\int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla \left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^y_i\phi_i \right) \cdot \nabla v_y \, d\Omega= \\[10pt]
&= \left[\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^x_i \cdot \int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla \phi_i \cdot \nabla v_x \, d\Omega\right] + 
\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^y_i \cdot \int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla \phi_i \cdot \nabla v_y \, d\Omega\right] = \\[10pt]
&=\left|\substack{\displaystyle\text{We now set $v_x(x,y) = \phi_j(x,y)$} \\ \displaystyle\text{and $v_y(x,y) = \phi_j(x,y)$}}\right|=\\[10pt]
&=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^x_i \cdot \int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla \phi_i \cdot \nabla \phi_j \, d\Omega\right] + 
\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^y_i \cdot \int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla \phi_i \cdot \nabla \phi_j \, d\Omega\right]=\\[10pt]
&=\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}A_{ji} u^x_i  + 
\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}A_{ji} u^y_i 
= \mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{u}_x + \mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{u}_y 
= \left|\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}^T\right| = \\[10pt]
&= \mathbf{A} \mathbf{u}_x + \mathbf{A} \mathbf{u}_y
=\left[ \begin{array}{cc}
    \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{A}
\end{array}\right] 
\begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{u}_x\\
    \mathbf{u}_y
\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
\]

\vspace{1.5em}
\subsubsection*{Local Matrix}

Now using the derived coordinate transformation, performed with the help of the Jacobian, we can calculate the local matrix $\mathbf{A}^{loc} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$

\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{A}_{ij} &= 
    \int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla \phi_i(x,y) \cdot \nabla \phi_j(x,y) \, d\Omega \\[10pt] 
    \mathbf{A}^{loc}_{ij} &=\left|
    \begin{array}{c}
         \nabla \phi(x,y) = \mathbf{J}_T^{-T}\nabla \hat{\phi}(\xi_1,\xi_2)\\
         d\Omega = dxdy=\left|\mathbf{J}_T\right|d\xi_1d\xi_2
    \end{array}
    \right| = \\[10pt]
    &= \int_{\hat{T}} \nu \left(\mathbf{J}_T^{-T}\nabla \hat{\phi}_i(\xi_1,\xi_2)\right)^{T} \left(\mathbf{J}_T^{-T}\nabla \hat{\phi}_j(\xi_1,\xi_2)\right) \left|\mathbf{J}_T\right| \, d\xi_1d\xi_2 = 
    \int_{\hat{T}} \nu \left(\nabla \hat{\phi}_i\right)^T\mathbf{J}^{-1}_T\mathbf{J}^{-T}_T \left(\nabla \hat{\phi}_j\right)\left|\mathbf{J}_T\right| \, d\xi_1d\xi_2 = \\[10pt]
    &= \int_{\hat{T}} \frac{\nu}{2\left|\mathbf{J}_T\right|} \left(\nabla \hat{\phi}_i\right)^T
    \begin{bmatrix}
    y_3 - y_1    & -(x_3 - x_1) \\
    -(y_2 - y_1) & x_2 - x_1
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
    y_3 - y_1    & -(y_2 - y_1) \\
    -(x_3 - x_1) & x_2 - x_1
    \end{bmatrix} 
    \left(\nabla \hat{\phi}_j\right)\left|\mathbf{J}_T\right| \, d\xi_1d\xi_2 = \\[10pt]
    &= \frac{\nu}{\left|\mathbf{J}_T\right|} \int_{\hat{T}} \left(\nabla \hat{\phi}_i\right)^T \mathbf{Q} \left(\nabla \hat{\phi}_j\right) \, d\xi_1d\xi_2 
    = \frac{\nu}{2\left|\mathbf{J}_T\right|} \left(\nabla \hat{\phi}_i\right)^T \mathbf{Q} \left(\nabla \hat{\phi}_j\right)
\end{aligned}
\]

\vspace{1.5ex}

After calculating the vector-matrix-vector products explicitly using the constant reference gradients \quad $\nabla \hat{\phi}_1 = [-1, -1]^T$, $\nabla \hat{\phi}_2 = [1, 0]^T$, and $\nabla \hat{\phi}_3 = [0, 1]^T$, we obtain the fully expanded local element matrix:

\vspace{1.5ex}

\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{A}^{loc} &= \frac{\nu}{\left|\mathbf{J}_T\right|}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \nabla \hat{\phi}_1\mathbf{Q}\nabla \hat{\phi}_1 & 
        \nabla \hat{\phi}_1\mathbf{Q}\nabla \hat{\phi}_2 &
        \nabla \hat{\phi}_1\mathbf{Q}\nabla \hat{\phi}_3 \\[10pt]
        \nabla \hat{\phi}_2\mathbf{Q}\nabla \hat{\phi}_1 &
        \nabla \hat{\phi}_2\mathbf{Q}\nabla \hat{\phi}_2 &
        \nabla \hat{\phi}_2\mathbf{Q}\nabla \hat{\phi}_3 \\[10pt]
        \nabla \hat{\phi}_3\mathbf{Q}\nabla \hat{\phi}_1 &
        \nabla \hat{\phi}_3\mathbf{Q}\nabla \hat{\phi}_2 &
        \nabla \hat{\phi}_3\mathbf{Q}\nabla \hat{\phi}_3 \\
    \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3} , \quad \text{where:}
\end{aligned}
\]

\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{Q} &= 
    % Cf(\mathbf{J}_T)^T Cf(\mathbf{J}_T) = 
    \mathbf{J}_T^{-1}\mathbf{J}_T^{-T} = \begin{bmatrix}
        (y_3 - y_1)^2 + (x_3 - x_1)^2 & 
        (y_1 - y_2)(y_3 - y_1) + (x_2 - x_1)(x_1 - x_3) \\
        (y_1 - y_2)(y_3 - y_1) + (x_2 - x_1)(x_1 - x_3) & 
        (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (x_2 - x_1)^2
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
\]

\vspace{1.5ex}

Please note that the matrices $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{A}^{loc}$ are symmetric. This structural property is highly advantageous for optimizing numerical calculations.

\subsection{Pressure Matrices \texorpdfstring{$\mathbf{B}_x$ and $\mathbf{B}_y$}{Bx and By} on the \texorpdfstring{$P_1$-iso-$P_1$}{P1-iso-P1} Macro-Element}

    \label{fig:p1-iso-p1-general-visual}
\end{wrapfigure}

Unlike a conforming equal-order pairing, the pressure basis function $\psi_j$ is supported on the \textit{macro} triangle $T^c \in \mathcal{T}_H$, while the velocity basis functions $\phi_i$ are supported on the four \textit{child} triangles $T^f_1,T^f_2,T^f_3,T^f_4 \in \mathcal{T}_h$ obtained by connecting the edge midpoints of $T^c$. Consequently the coupling integral must be split into a sum over the four children, and the pressure basis function must be re-expressed in each child's own local reference coordinates. This restriction is the main idea of the $P_1$-iso-$P_1$ assembly.

The matrices $\mathbf{B}_x$ and $\mathbf{B}_y$ are the discrete counterpart of the divergence operator: together they enforce incompressibility by coupling every pressure degree of freedom to the velocity field's local rate of expansion or contraction, in exactly the same way the continuous operator $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$ couples pressure to velocity through the incompressibility constraint. Where $\mathbf{A}$ measures how velocity varies against itself, $\mathbf{B}_x,\mathbf{B}_y$ measure how velocity varies against pressure, and it is this cross-coupling that gives the assembled system its saddle-point structure. As with $\mathbf{A}$, their construction starts directly from the corresponding term in the weak formulation and proceeds by substituting the finite element expansion of the trial and test functions -- only here the added subtlety of the $P_1$-iso-$P_1$ macro-element geometry, described above, must be threaded through every step.

\subsubsection*{Weak formulation}

\begin{tcolorbox}[
    colframe=ForestGreen, 
    colback=ForestGreen!7!white, 
    sharp corners
]
\begin{center}
   $\displaystyle -\int_{\Omega} p(\nabla \cdot v) \, d\Omega$ 
\end{center}
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{align*}
-\int_{\Omega} p (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}) \, d\Omega &= \left| \begin{aligned} p(x,y) &\approx \sum_{j=1}^{N_p} p_j \psi_j \\ \mathbf{v}(x,y) &= \begin{bmatrix} v_x(x,y) \\ v_y(x,y) \end{bmatrix} \end{aligned} \right| = -\int_{\Omega} \sum_{j=1}^{N_p} p_j \psi_j \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{bmatrix} \, d\Omega = \\[10pt]
&= \left[ \sum_{j=1}^{N_p} p_j \cdot \left( -\int_{\Omega} \psi_j \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} \, d\Omega \right) \right] + \left[ \sum_{j=1}^{N_p} p_j \cdot \left( -\int_{\Omega} \psi_j \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} \, d\Omega \right) \right] = \left| \begin{aligned} v_x(x,y) &= \phi_i(x,y) \\ v_y(x,y) &= \phi_i(x,y) \end{aligned} \right| = \\[10pt]
&= \sum_{j=1}^{N_p} p_j \cdot \left( -\int_{\Omega} \psi_j \frac{\partial \phi_i}{\partial x} \, d\Omega \right) + \sum_{j=1}^{N_p} p_j \cdot \left( -\int_{\Omega} \psi_j \frac{\partial \phi_i}{\partial y} \, d\Omega \right) = \sum_{j=1}^{N_p} p_j (B_x)_{ji} + \sum_{j=1}^{N_p} p_j (B_y)_{ji} = \\[10pt]
&= \mathbf{B}_x^T \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{B}_y^T \mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{B}_x^T & \mathbf{B}_y^T \end{bmatrix} 
\begin{bmatrix} 
    \mathbf{p} \\ 
    \mathbf{p} 
\end{bmatrix} 
\end{align*}

\subsubsection*{Local Matrix}

Summing the contribution from each of the four children, the local matrix entries are expressed as follows:
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \left(\mathbf{B}_x^{loc}\right)_{ij} 
    &= -\sum_{m=1}^{4} \int_{T^f_m} \psi_j \frac{\partial \phi_i}{\partial x} \, d\Omega = \left|
    \begin{array}{c}
         \frac{\partial \phi_i}{\partial x} = |\mathbf{J}_{T}|^{-1}\left((y_3 - y_1)\frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi _1} - (y_2 - y_1)\frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi _2}\right)\\[7pt]
         d\Omega = dxdy=\left|\mathbf{J}_{T}\right|d\xi_1d\xi_2
    \end{array}
    \right|=\\[10pt]
    &= -\sum_{m=1}^{4} \left[(y^m_3 - y^m_1)\frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi _1} - (y^m_2 - y^m_1)\frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi _2} \right]\int_{\hat{T}} \hat{\psi}_j \, d\xi_1d\xi_2 
    = -\sum_{m=1}^{4} \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{jm}  \left[(y^m_3 - y^m_1)\frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi _1} - (y^m_2 - y^m_1)\frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi _2} \right]
\end{aligned}
\]

Similarly:
\[
\left(\mathbf{B}_y^{loc}\right)_{ij} = -\sum_{m=1}^{4} \int_{T^f_m} \psi_j \frac{\partial \phi_i}{\partial y} \, d\Omega,
= -\sum_{m=1}^{4} \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{jm}  \left[-(x_3 - x_1)\frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi _1} + (x_2 - x_1)\frac{\partial \hat{\phi} _i}{\partial \xi _2}\right]
\]

\subsection{Load Vector \texorpdfstring{$\mathbf{F}$}{F} Assembly and Inhomogeneous Boundary Lifting}

The load vector $\mathbf{F}$ is the discrete counterpart of the right-hand side of the momentum equation in \eqref{eq:stokes_weak}. Unlike $\mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{B}_x$, and $\mathbf{B}_y$, which are bilinear forms coupling two finite element expansions, $\mathbf{F}$ is assembled from a \textit{linear} functional of the test function $v$ alone: it collects the prescribed Neumann traction on $\Gamma_N$ and, through the boundary lifting technique of \eqref{eq:stokes_weak}, the contribution of the known inhomogeneous Dirichlet data carried by $\mathbf{r}_g$. As with $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}_x,\mathbf{B}_y$, its construction starts directly from the corresponding term in the weak formulation and proceeds by substituting the finite element expansion of the test function.

\subsubsection*{Weak formulation}

\begin{tcolorbox}[
    colframe=ForestGreen, 
    colback=ForestGreen!7!white, 
    sharp corners
]
\begin{center}
   $\displaystyle \int_{\Gamma_N} h \cdot v \, d\Gamma - \int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla r_g : \nabla v \, d\Omega$
\end{center}
\end{tcolorbox}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\Gamma_N} \mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{v}\,d\Gamma - \int_{\Omega}\nu\nabla \mathbf{r}_g:\nabla \mathbf{v}\,d\Omega
&=\left|
\begin{array}{c}
    \mathbf{h}(x,y) = \begin{bmatrix} h_x(x,y) \\ h_y(x,y) \end{bmatrix}
    \qquad
    \mathbf{v}(x,y) = \begin{bmatrix} v_x(x,y) \\ v_y(x,y) \end{bmatrix}
    \qquad
    \mathbf{r}_g(x,y) = \begin{bmatrix} r_g^x(x,y) \\ r_g^y(x,y) \end{bmatrix}
\end{array}
\right| = \\[10pt]
&=\int_{\Gamma_N}\!\left(h_x v_x + h_y v_y\right)d\Gamma
- \int_{\Omega}\nu\left(\nabla r_g^x\cdot\nabla v_x + \nabla r_g^y\cdot\nabla v_y\right)d\Omega = \\[10pt]
&=\left[\int_{\Gamma_N}\! h_x v_x\,d\Gamma - \int_{\Omega}\nu\nabla r_g^x\cdot\nabla v_x\,d\Omega\right]
+\left[\int_{\Gamma_N}\! h_y v_y\,d\Gamma - \int_{\Omega}\nu\nabla r_g^y\cdot\nabla v_y\,d\Omega\right] =
\\[10pt]
&=\left|
\begin{array}{c}
    r_g^x(x,y) \approx \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_v} \left(\mathbf{r}_g^{\,x}\right)_i \phi_i(x,y)
    \qquad
    r_g^y(x,y) \approx \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_v} \left(\mathbf{r}_g^{\,y}\right)_i \phi_i(x,y)
\end{array}
\right| = \\[10pt]
&=\left[\int_{\Gamma_N}\! h_x v_x\,d\Gamma - \sum_{i=1}^{N_v}\left(\mathbf{r}_g^{\,x}\right)_i\int_{\Omega}\nu\nabla \phi_i\cdot\nabla v_x\,d\Omega\right]
+\left[\cdots\right]_{y} = \\[10pt]
&=\left|\substack{\displaystyle\text{We now set $v_x(x,y) = \phi_j(x,y)$} \\ \displaystyle\text{and $v_y(x,y) = \phi_j(x,y)$}}\right| = \\[10pt]
&=\left[\int_{\Gamma_N}\! h_x \phi_j\,d\Gamma - \sum_{i=1}^{N_v}\left(\mathbf{r}_g^{\,x}\right)_i A_{ji}\right]
+\left[\int_{\Gamma_N}\! h_y \phi_j\,d\Gamma - \sum_{i=1}^{N_v}\left(\mathbf{r}_g^{\,y}\right)_i A_{ji}\right] = \\[10pt]
&=\left|\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}^T\right| = \left(\mathbf{F}_{forces}^{\,x} - \mathbf{A}\vec{r}_g^{\,x}\right)_j
+\left(\mathbf{F}_{forces}^{\,y} - \mathbf{A}\vec{r}_g^{\,y}\right)_j
\end{aligned}
\]

\vspace{1ex}
where:

\[
\left(\mathbf{F}_{forces}^{\,x}\right)_j = \int_{\Gamma_N} h_x\,\phi_j\,d\Gamma 
\qquad \qquad \qquad
\left(\mathbf{F}_{forces}^{\,y}\right)_j = \int_{\Gamma_N} h_y\,\phi_j\,d\Gamma
\]

are the discrete Neumann traction loads, and the second term in each bracket is exactly the stiffness matrix $\mathbf{A}$ applied to the known lifting coefficients, since it is the same bilinear form $\int_\Omega \nu\nabla\phi_i\cdot\nabla\phi_j\,d\Omega$ evaluated once with the trial index attached to $\mathbf{r}_g$ rather than to an unknown $\mathbf{u}$. Stacking the $x$- and $y$-components gives the final, compact result:

\[
\mathbf{F} = \int_{\Gamma_N} h \cdot v \, d\Gamma - \int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla r_g : \nabla v \, d\Omega = \mathbf{F}_{forces} - 
\begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{0} \\
    \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{A}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{r}_g^x \\
    \mathbf{r}_g^y
\end{bmatrix}= \mathbf{F}_{forces} - \mathbf{M}_v \mathbf{r}_g
\]

During the further calculations no Neumann boundary conditions are enforced, so the expression simplifies further to just:

\[
\mathbf{F} = - \mathbf{M}_v\mathbf{r}_g
\]

\subsection{Saddle-Point Block Matrix \texorpdfstring{$\mathbf{K}$}{K}}

By organizing the viscous diffusion blocks and the divergence constraint blocks into a single monolithic operator, we construct the global saddle-point system. This specific structural arrangement takes the classic Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) form:

% Pressure acts like a lagrange multiplier!!!

\[
\left[
\begin{array}{ccc}
\mathbf{A} & 0 & \mathbf{B}_x^T \\
0 & \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{B}_y^T \\
\mathbf{B}_x & \mathbf{B}_y & 0
\end{array}
\right]
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{u}_x \\
\mathbf{u}_y \\
\mathbf{p}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{F}_x \\
\mathbf{F}_y \\
\mathbf{0}
\end{bmatrix}
\]

The block layout reflects the underlying \textbf{mixed formulation}~\eqref{eq:stokes_weak}, where the diagonal velocity stiffness blocks $\mathbf{A}$ remain symmetric positive definite, while the zero block on the lower diagonal highlights the lack of an explicit pressure time-dependence or compressibility stabilizer. The dimensional layout of these individual blocks scales according to the total degrees of freedom allocated to each field variable:

\[
\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_u \times N_u} \qquad
\mathbf{B}_x \in \mathbb{R}^{N_p \times N_u} \qquad
\mathbf{B}_x^T \in \mathbb{R}^{N_u \times N_p}
\]

\subsection{Load Vector \texorpdfstring{$\mathbf{F}$}{F} Assembly and Inhomogeneous Boundary Lifting}

\[
\mathbf{F} = \int_{\Gamma_N} h \cdot v \, d\Gamma - \int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla r_g : \nabla v \, d\Omega = \mathbf{F}_{forces} - \mathbf{A}\vec{r_g} = 
\left[
    \begin{array}{c}
    F_x\\
    F_y
    \end{array}
\right] - \mathbf{A}\vec{r_g}
\]

\subsubsection*{Construction of the Lifting Function \texorpdfstring{$\mathbf{r}_g$}{rg}}

The strong Dirichlet condition reads $\mathbf u = \mathbf g$ on $\Gamma_D$, and the weak formulation is stated for the homogeneous unknown $\mathbf u \in X$ via the decomposition
$\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}_h + \mathbf{r}_g$. Discretely, $\mathbf{r}_g \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_v}$ is simply the nodal interpolant of $\mathbf g$. For every fine-mesh node $X_i$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lifting_function}
\left(\mathbf{r}_g^{\,x}\right)_i =
\begin{cases}
    g_x(X_i) & X_i \in \Gamma_D^{\text{inlet}}, \\[4pt]
    0 & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad
\forall i, \,\left(\mathbf{r}_g^{\,y}\right)_i = 0
\end{equation}

\vspace{1.5em}
Specifically, the horizontal inflow velocity $g_x$ is prescribed as a parameterized Poiseuille parabolic profile across the inlet segment $[y_1, y_2]$:

\[
g_x(y) = -\alpha (y - y_1)(y - y_2),
\]

where $\alpha > 0$ controls the peak inflow amplitude, and $g_x(y_1) = g_x(y_2) = 0$ enforces zero velocity at the boundaries of the inlet. Walls carry the homogeneous no-slip value $\mathbf g = \mathbf 0$ automatically, since $\mathbf{r}_g$ is zero everywhere it is not explicitly prescribed. Finally, the outlet is left as the traction-free boundary $\Gamma_N$, so $\mathbf h = \mathbf 0$ and $\mathbf F_{\text{forces}} = \mathbf 0$ in subsequent formulations.

The downstream outlet red boundary nodes remain unconstrained for traction-free outflow.}

\begin{color}{blue}
\subsubsection*{Momentum and Continuity Right-Hand Sides}

With $\mathbf F_{forces}=\mathbf 0$, the momentum block reduces directly to $\mathbf F = -\mathbf A\vec r_g$
as stated above. The continuity equation of the weak system~\eqref{eq:stokes_weak} needs the same treatment:
testing $-\int_\Omega q(\nabla\cdot \mathbf u)\,d\Omega = \int_\Omega q(\nabla\cdot \mathbf r_g)\,d\Omega$
against the discrete pressure basis and applying the identity of \S5.2.1 to \emph{both} sides (once for the
homogeneous unknown $\mathbf u$, once for the known lift $\vec r_g$) gives
\[
-\mathbf p^T \mathbf B \mathbf u = \mathbf p^T \mathbf B \vec r_g \quad \forall \mathbf p
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathbf B_x \mathbf u_x + \mathbf B_y \mathbf u_y = -\left(\mathbf B_x \vec r_g^{\,x} + \mathbf B_y \vec r_g^{\,y}\right).
\]
Assembling all three rows, the full right-hand side of the lifted saddle-point system is
\[
\left[
\begin{array}{c}
\mathbf F_x \\ \mathbf F_y \\ \mathbf 0
\end{array}
\right]
=
\left[
\begin{array}{c}
-\mathbf A \vec r_g^{\,x} \\[4pt]
-\mathbf A \vec r_g^{\,y} \\[4pt]
-\left(\mathbf B_x \vec r_g^{\,x} + \mathbf B_y \vec r_g^{\,y}\right)
\end{array}
\right].
\]

\subsubsection*{Enforcing the Homogeneous Dirichlet Condition}

Because $\mathbf u \in X$ requires $\mathbf u = \mathbf 0$ exactly on $\Gamma_D$, every degree of freedom $i \in \Gamma_D$ must be pinned to zero in the linear system itself, not merely approximated. This is done by row substitution: for each such $i$, row $i$ of $\mathbf K$ is replaced by the $i$-th unit row and $F_i$ is set to zero,

\[
\mathbf K_{i,:} \leftarrow \mathbf e_i^T, \qquad F_i \leftarrow 0,
\]
which forces $u_i = 0$ directly upon solving. Crucially, only the \emph{row} is overwritten — the column
$\mathbf K_{:,i}$ is left untouched. This is exact precisely because the imposed value is zero: for every other
equation $j$, the term $\mathbf K_{j,i}\, u_i = \mathbf K_{j,i}\cdot 0 = 0$ automatically, so no compensating
correction to $F_j$ is required. This is the payoff of lifting in the first place — had the original
inhomogeneous problem been assembled directly for $\mathbf u_{total}$, enforcing $u_{total,i}=g_i\neq 0$ this
way would require subtracting $\mathbf K_{j,i}\,g_i$ from every other row's right-hand side; working with the
homogeneous unknown $\mathbf u$ instead reduces Dirichlet enforcement to the cheapest possible operation.

\subsection{Pressure Null-Space Regulation and Singular Constraints}

Pressure enters the momentum equation only through the coupling matrices $\mathbf B_x^T,\mathbf B_y^T$, and in
this formulation the outlet is left as a natural, do-nothing boundary rather than one carrying an explicit
pressure reference. As a result, nothing in the assembled system fixes an absolute pressure level: the discrete
saddle-point matrix $\mathbf K$ is singular (or, numerically, severely ill-conditioned) in its pressure block,
exactly as in a fully enclosed cavity flow, and a direct sparse solve of $\mathbf K\vec s = \mathbf F$ is not
well-posed as stated.

The remedy is the same row-substitution technique used for the velocity Dirichlet nodes, applied to a single
pressure degree of freedom $p_{\text{ref}}$ chosen at (or nearest to) the outlet:
\[
\mathbf K_{p_{\text{ref}},:} \leftarrow \mathbf e_{p_{\text{ref}}}^T, \qquad F_{p_{\text{ref}}} \leftarrow 0,
\]
which fixes $p_{p_{\text{ref}}} = 0$ and removes the indeterminacy, leaving every other pressure value defined
relative to this reference. As before, only the row is overwritten: the column $\mathbf K_{:,p_{\text{ref}}}$
is preserved so that the (now fixed, zero) reference pressure still correctly participates in every other
momentum equation, by the same zero-contribution argument as above. If the mesh has no coarse node lying
exactly on the outlet boundary, the reference is instead taken as the coarse pressure node nearest to the
outlet's midpoint, ensuring the regularization is always well-defined regardless of mesh irregularity.

\end{color}

\newpage
\section{Ladyzhenskaya–Babuška–Brezzi Condition}
In numerical partial differential equations, the \textit{Ladyzhenskaya–Babuška–Brezzi }(LBB) condition is a sufficient condition for a saddle point problem to have a unique solution that depends continuously on the input data.

At the continuous level, the velocity space $V$ and pressure space $Q$ must satisfy:

\begin{tcolorbox}[
    colframe=heaven, 
    colback=heaven!7!white, 
    sharp corners
]
\begin{center}
\begin{align*}
    \exists \beta>0: \quad
    \inf_{q \in Q_{\setminus\{0\}}} \space \sup_{v \in V_{\setminus\{0\}}} \left(\frac{\int_{\Omega}q(\nabla \cdot v)d\Omega}{\|v\|_{V}\cdot\|q\|_{Q}}\right) \ge \beta 
\end{align*}
\end{center}
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{Discrete Mapping and Coordinate Representation}
For this problem it is useful to reformulate the condition in a discrete way. We can start by mapping the continuous functions to their nodal coordinate representations:


\[
v \rightarrow \mathbf{u}=
\begin{bmatrix}
    u_1^x\\
    \vdots\\
    u_{N_v}^x\\
    u_1^y\\
    \vdots\\
    u_{N_v}^y
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_v},
\quad 
q \rightarrow \mathbf{p}=
\begin{bmatrix}
    p_1\\
    \vdots\\
    p_{N_p}
\end{bmatrix}\in \mathbb{R}^{N_p}\\[10pt]
\]
The corresponding discrete differential bilinear forms translate directly into the global block matrices assembled across the mesh:

\small
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
    \displaystyle
    \mathbf{A}_{ij} = \int_{\Omega} \nu \nabla \phi_i(x,y) \cdot \nabla \phi_j(x,y) \, d\Omega \quad & 
    \displaystyle \left(\mathbf{B}_x\right)_{ij} = -\int_{\Omega} \psi_j(x,y)\frac{\partial \phi_i(x,y)}{\partial x} \, d\Omega \quad & 
    \displaystyle \left(\mathbf{B}_y\right)_{ij} = -\int_{\Omega} \psi_j(x,y) \frac{\partial \phi_i(x,y)}{\partial y} \, d\Omega \\[15pt]
    \displaystyle \mathbf{B}=
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{B}_x & \mathbf{B}_y
    \end{bmatrix}
    \quad & \displaystyle \mathbf{M}_v=
\begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{0} \\
    \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{A}
\end{bmatrix}
\quad & \displaystyle \left(\mathbf{M}_p\right)_{ij} = \int_{\Omega} \psi_j(x,y)\psi_i(x,y)\, d\Omega
\end{array}
\]
\normalsize

Where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_v}$ is the velocity vector on a fine grid;
    \item $\mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_p}$ is the pressure vector on a coarse grid;
    \item $\mathbf{B}_x \in \mathbb{R}^{N_p \times N_v}$ is the discrete horizontal divergence operator;
    \item $\mathbf{B}_y \in \mathbb{R}^{N_p \times N_v}$ is the discrete vertical divergence operator;
    \item $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_p \times 2N_v}$ is a discrete divergence operator, responsible for coupling pressure and velocity;
    \item $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_v \times N_v}$ is a discrete Laplacian operator that handles the viscosity of the fluid;
    \item $\mathbf{M}_v \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_v \times 2N_v}$ is the velocity energy weight matrix, used to calculate the physical size and kinetic energy of the velocity field.
    \item $\mathbf{M}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{N_p \times N_p}$ is the pressure mass matrix that computes the spatial overlap of pressure basis functions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Discretization Proofs for Operators and Field Norms}
Using the coordinate definitions above, we can systematically transform the three core components of the continuous inf-sup condition into algebraic matrix operations:

\begingroup
\small
\begin{enumerate}
    \raggedright
    % Write out in Bx and By and then combine and v_x, v_y have to be used
    \item $\begin{aligned}[t]
        \displaystyle\int_{\Omega}q(\nabla \cdot v) \, d\Omega &= \left|
        \begin{array}{ccc}
            q(x,y)\approx \displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_p}p_j\psi_j(x,y) & v_x(x,y)\approx \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^x_i\phi_i(x,y) & \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x}\approx \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^x_i\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi_i(x,y)\\
            v(x,y)=
            \begin{bmatrix}
               v_x(x,y) \\
               v_y(x,y)  
            \end{bmatrix} & v_y(x,y)\approx \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^y_i\phi_i(x,y) & \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y}\approx \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^y_i\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\phi_i(x,y)
        \end{array}\right| = \\[10pt]
        &= \int_{\Omega}q(x,y) \left(
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\\
            \frac{\partial}{\partial y}
        \end{bmatrix}^T
        \begin{bmatrix}
            v_x(x,y) \\
            v_y(x,y)
        \end{bmatrix}
        \right) \, d\Omega 
        = \int_{\Omega} q(x,y)\left( \frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} \right) \, d\Omega = \\[10pt]
        &= \int_{\Omega} q(x,y)\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x} \, d\Omega
        + \int_{\Omega} q(x,y)\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y} \, d\Omega = \\[10pt]
        &= \int_{\Omega} \sum_{j=1}^{N_p}p_j\psi_j(x,y) \sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^x_i\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi_i(x,y) \, d\Omega + \int_{\Omega} \sum_{j=1}^{N_p}p_j\psi_j(x,y) \sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u^y_i\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\phi_i(x,y) \, d\Omega = \\[10pt]
        &= \left[\sum_{j=1}^{N_p}\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}p_j \cdot \int_{\Omega}\psi_j(x,y) \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\phi_i(x,y) \, d\Omega \cdot u^x_i \right] + \left[ \sum_{j=1}^{N_p}\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}p_j \cdot \int_{\Omega}\psi_j(x,y) \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\phi_i(x,y) \, d\Omega \cdot u^y_i \right] \\[10pt]
        &= \sum_{j=1}^{N_p}\sum_{i=1}^{N_v} p_j \left(B_x\right)_{ji} u^x_i + \sum_{j=1}^{N_p}\sum_{i=1}^{N_v} p_j\left(B_y\right)_{ji} u^y_i = \mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{B}_x \mathbf{u}_x + \mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{B}_y \mathbf{u}_y = \mathbf{p}^T \left[\mathbf{B}_x \quad \mathbf{B}_y\right]
        \begin{bmatrix}
            \mathbf{u}_x \\
            \mathbf{u}_y
        \end{bmatrix} = \\[10pt]
        &=\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}
        \end{aligned}$

    \item $\begin{aligned}[t]
    \|v\|_V &= \left( \displaystyle\int_{\Omega} \left( \|\nabla v_x\|^2 + \|\nabla v_y\|^2 \right) \, d\Omega \right)^{1/2}
    = \left( \int_{\Omega} \left( \nabla v_x \cdot \nabla v_x + \nabla v_y \cdot \nabla v_y \right) \, d\Omega \right)^{1/2} =\\
    &= 
    \left|
    \begin{array}{cc}
         v_x(x,y) \approx \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u_i^x\phi_i(x,y) & \nabla v_x(x,y) \approx \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u_i^x\nabla\phi_i(x,y)\\[10pt]
         \quad v_y(x,y) \approx \displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_v}u_j^y\phi_j(x,y) & \nabla v_y(x,y) \approx \displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_v}u_j^y\nabla\phi_j(x,y)
    \end{array}
    \right| =\\
    &= \left( \displaystyle\int_{\Omega} \left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u_i^x\nabla\phi_i(x,y) \cdot \sum_{j=1}^{N_v}u_j^x\nabla\phi_j(x,y) + \sum_{i=1}^{N_v}u_i^y\nabla\phi_i(x,y) \cdot \sum_{j=1}^{N_v}u_j^y\nabla\phi_j(x,y) \right) \, d\Omega \right)^{1/2} =\\
    &= \left(\left[ \sum_{i=1}^{N_v}\sum_{j=1}^{N_v} u_i^x \cdot \displaystyle\int_{\Omega} \nabla\phi_i(x,y) \cdot \nabla\phi_j(x,y)\, d\Omega \cdot u_j^x\right] + \sum_{i=1}^{N_v}\sum_{j=1}^{N_v}\left[u_i^y\cdot \displaystyle\int_{\Omega} \nabla\phi_i(x,y) \cdot \nabla\phi_j(x,y)\, d\Omega \cdot u_j^y\right]\right)^{1/2} = \\[10pt]
    &= \displaystyle\left(\left[ \sum_{i=1}^{N_v}\sum_{j=1}^{N_v} u_i^x A_{ij}  u_j^x\right] + \left[\sum_{i=1}^{N_v}\sum_{j=1}^{N_v}u_i^y A_{ij} u_j^y\right]\right)^{1/2} = \\[10pt]
    &= \left(\mathbf{u_x}^T \mathbf{A} \mathbf{u_x} + \mathbf{u_y}^T \mathbf{A} \mathbf{u_y}\right)^{1/2} = \left(
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{u_x}\\
        \mathbf{u_y}
    \end{bmatrix}^T
    \left[
    \begin{array}{cc}
        \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{0} \\
        \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{A}
    \end{array}
    \right]
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{u_x}\\
        \mathbf{u_y}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \right)^{1/2}
    = \left(\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{M}_v \mathbf{u}\right)^{1/2}
    \end{aligned}$
    
    \item $\begin{aligned}[t]
        \|q\|_Q &= \left( \int_{\Omega} q(x,y)^2 \, d\Omega \right)^{1/2} = \left|q(x,y) \approx \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_p}p_i\psi_i(x,y)\right|=\\
        &= \left( \int_{\Omega} \sum_{i=1}^{N_p}p_i\psi_i(x,y) \cdot \sum_{j=1}^{N_p}p_j\psi_j(x,y)\, d\Omega \right)^{1/2} = \left( \int_{\Omega} \sum_{i=1}^{N_p}\sum_{j=1}^{N_p}p_i p_j\psi_j(x,y)\psi_i(x,y)\, d\Omega \right)^{1/2} = \\
        &= \left( \sum_{i=1}^{N_p}\sum_{j=1}^{N_p} p_i \cdot \left(\int_{\Omega} \psi_j(x,y)\psi_i(x,y)\, d\Omega\right) \cdot p_j\right)^{1/2} = \left( \sum_{i=1}^{N_p}\sum_{j=1}^{N_p} p_i \cdot \left(M_p\right)_{ij} \cdot p_j\right)^{1/2} = \left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{1/2}
    \end{aligned}$
\end{enumerate}
\normalsize

\subsection{The Final Discrete Matrix Form}
By substituting our three derived algebraic expressions directly back into the continuous supremum framework, the continuous inf-sup condition simplifies into its final, discrete matrix form:

\[
\exists \beta>0: \quad
\inf_{\mathbf{p} \not= \mathbf{0}} \space \sup_{\mathbf{u} \not= \mathbf{0}} \left(\frac{\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}}{\left(\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{M}_v \mathbf{u}\right)^{1/2} \cdot \left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{1/2}}\right) \ge \beta \\[10pt]
\]

This discrete fraction acts as a normalized compatibility test, using $\mathbf{M}_v$ and $\mathbf{M}_p$ to ensure that the fields are measured by their true physical sizes rather than arbitrary numbers. Because calculating a nested maximum and minimum over thousands of mesh nodes is impossible for a computer, we must use a mathematical shortcut to transform this fraction into a simple matrix eigenvalue problem.

\subsection{Solving the Optimization Problem}
The optimization problem is of the following appearence:

\[
\min_{\mathbf{p} \not= \mathbf{0}} \space \max_{\mathbf{u} \not= \mathbf{0}} \left(\frac{\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}}{\left(\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{M}_v \mathbf{u}\right)^{1/2} \cdot \left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{1/2}}\right)\\[10pt]
\]

We start with the maximization of with respect to $\mathbf{u}$:

\small
\begin{align*}
    \max_{\mathbf{u} \not= \mathbf{0}} \left(\frac{\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}}{\left(\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{M}_v \mathbf{u}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \cdot \left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right) 
    &= \frac{1}{\left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}} \max_{\mathbf{u} \not= \mathbf{0}} \left(\frac{\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}}{\left(\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{M}_v \mathbf{u}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right) = \left|
    \begin{array}{c}
       \mathbf{M}_v = \mathbf{M}_v^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{M}_v^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
       \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{M}_v^{\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{u}, \quad \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{w}
    \end{array}
    \right| = \\[10pt]
    &= \frac{1}{\left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}} 
    \max_{\mathbf{w} \not= \mathbf{0}} \left(\frac{\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{B} \left(\mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{w}\right)}{\left(\left(\mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{w}\right)^T \mathbf{M}_v \left(\mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{w}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right) = \\[10pt]
    &= \frac{1}{\left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}} 
    \max_{\mathbf{w} \not= \mathbf{0}} \left(\frac{\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{w}}{\left( \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{M}_v^{\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{M}_v^{\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{w}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right) = \\[10pt]
    &= \frac{1}{\left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}} 
    \max_{\mathbf{w} \not= \mathbf{0}} \frac{\left( \mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{p} \right)^T \mathbf{w}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_2}
    = \left| 
    \begin{array}{c}
        \mathbf{a} = c \mathbf{x}\\
        \displaystyle
        \max_{\mathbf{a} \ne \mathbf{0}}\frac{\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{a}}{\|\mathbf{a}\|_2} =
        \displaystyle 
        \max_{\mathbf{x} \ne \mathbf{0}}\frac{c \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{x}}{c\|\mathbf{x}\|_2}
        =\|\mathbf{x}\|_2         
    \end{array}
    \right| = \\[10pt]
    &= \frac{1}{\left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}
   \lVert\mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{p}\rVert_2
\end{align*}
\normalsize

With the maximization out of the way it is now possible to minimize over pressure $\mathbf{p}$:

\small
\begin{align*}
    \min_{\mathbf{p} \ne \mathbf{0}} \frac{
   \lVert\mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{p}\rVert_2 }{\left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}
   \quad
   &\longrightarrow\quad
   \left(\min_{\mathbf{p} \ne \mathbf{0}} \frac{
   \lVert\mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{p}\rVert_2 }{\left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}
   \right)^2
   = \min_{\mathbf{p} \ne \mathbf{0}} \frac{
   \left(\mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{p}\right)^T \left(\mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{p}\right)}{\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}} = \\[10pt]
   &= \min_{\mathbf{p} \ne \mathbf{0}} \frac{
   \mathbf{p}^T\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}}
   = \min_{\mathbf{p} \ne \mathbf{0}} \frac{\mathbf{p}^T\left(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}_v^{-1}\mathbf{B}^T\right)\mathbf{p}}{\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}} 
   = 
   \left|
   \begin{array}{c}
        R(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}; \mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}} \\[5pt]
        \mathbf{q} = \mathbf{M}_p^{1/2}\mathbf{p} \\
        \mathbf{p} = \mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\mathbf{q}
   \end{array}
   \right| = \\[10pt]
   &= \min_{\mathbf{q} \ne \mathbf{0}} \frac{\left(\mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\mathbf{q} \right)^T\left(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}_v^{-1}\mathbf{B}^T\right)\left(\mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\mathbf{q} \right)}{\left(\mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\mathbf{q} \right)^T \mathbf{M}_p \left(\mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\mathbf{q} \right)} 
   = \min_{\mathbf{q} \ne \mathbf{0}} \frac{\mathbf{q}^T\left(\mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}_v^{-1}\mathbf{B}^T\mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\right)\mathbf{q}}{\mathbf{q}^T\mathbf{q}} = \\[10pt]  
   &= \left|
   \begin{array}{c}
        \text{Rayleigh quotient:}\\
        R(\mathbf{K};\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{K}\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x}}\\[5pt]
        \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}_v^{-1}\mathbf{B}^T\mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}
   \end{array}
   \right| 
   = \min_{\mathbf{q} \ne \mathbf{0}} R(\mathbf{K};\mathbf{q}) 
   = \lambda_{min}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)
\end{align*}
\normalsize

To compute minimum of the Rayleigh quotient we would need to compute its minimal eigenvalue and get rid of the substitution $\mathbf{q}$:

\small
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{K}\mathbf{q}
    &=\lambda\mathbf{q}\\[10pt]
    \mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}_p^{1/2}\mathbf{p}
    &=\lambda\mathbf{M}_p^{1/2}\mathbf{p} \quad \Big| \cdot \mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\\    \mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}_p^{1/2}\mathbf{p}
    &=\lambda\mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\mathbf{M}_p^{1/2}\mathbf{p}\\    \mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\left(\mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}_v^{-1}\mathbf{B}^T\mathbf{M}_p^{-1/2}\right)\mathbf{M}_p^{1/2}\mathbf{p}
    &=\lambda\mathbf{p}\\    \left(\mathbf{M}_p^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}_v^{-1}\mathbf{B}^T\right)\mathbf{p}
    &=\lambda\mathbf{p}\\[10pt]    \lambda_{min}\left(\hat{\mathbf{K}}\right)&, \quad \text{where: }\hat{\mathbf{K}} = \mathbf{M}_p^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}_v^{-1}\mathbf{B}^T
\end{align*}
\normalsize

We also have to revert the squaring of the term used earlier:

\small
\begin{align*}
   \left(\min_{\mathbf{p} \ne 0} \frac{
   \lVert\mathbf{M}_v^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathbf{B}^T \mathbf{p}\rVert_2 }{\left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}} \right)^2
   &= \lambda_{min}\left(\hat{\mathbf{K}}\right)\\[10pt]
   \min_{\mathbf{p} \not= \mathbf{0}} \space \max_{\mathbf{u} \not= \mathbf{0}} \left(\frac{\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}}{\left(\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{M}_v \mathbf{u}\right)^{1/2} \cdot \left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{1/2}}\right)&= \sqrt{\lambda_{min}\left(\hat{\mathbf{K}}\right)}\\[10pt]
\end{align*}
\normalsize

\subsection{Numerical Stability Assessment}
Lastly, we can look at the stability condition in its final, practical form. In a two-dimensional simulation, the velocity field is split into horizontal and vertical components. Because of this, the global divergence matrix $\mathbf{B}$ and the velocity matrix $\mathbf{M}_v$ must be expanded into their individual block components $\mathbf{B}_x$, $\mathbf{B}_y$, and the velocity stiffness matrix $\mathbf{A}$. 

Substituting these block matrices into our eigenvalue problem yields the fully expanded discrete inf-sup condition:

\begin{align*}
    \exists \beta>0: \quad
    \inf_{\mathbf{p} \not= \mathbf{0}} \space \sup_{\mathbf{u} \not= \mathbf{0}} \left(\frac{\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{u}}{\left(\mathbf{u}^T \mathbf{M}_v \mathbf{u}\right)^{1/2} \cdot \left(\mathbf{p}^T \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}\right)^{1/2}}\right) 
    = \sqrt{\lambda_{min}\left(\hat{\mathbf{K}}\right)}
    &\ge \beta \\[10pt]
    \exists \beta>0: \quad    \sqrt{\lambda_{min}\left(\mathbf{M}_p^{-1}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}_v^{-1}\mathbf{B}^T\right)} 
    = \sqrt{\lambda_{min}\left(\mathbf{M}_p^{-1}    
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{B}_x & \mathbf{B}_y
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{0} \\
        \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{A}
    \end{bmatrix}^{-1}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{B}_x^T\\
        \mathbf{B}_y^T
    \end{bmatrix}    
    \right)}
    &\ge \beta\\[10pt]  
    \exists \beta>0: \quad
    \sqrt{\lambda_{min}\left(\mathbf{M}_p^{-1}\left(
    \mathbf{B}_x \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B}_x^T
    + \mathbf{B}_y \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B}_y^T
    \right)\right)}
    &\ge \beta \\[10pt]      
\end{align*}

The minimum eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min}$ serves as a direct numerical test for the stability of our chosen velocity and pressure finite element grids. To verify if a grid combination satisfies the discrete inf-sup condition, we look at how $\lambda_{\min}$ behaves as the mesh is refined:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stable Elements:} If the chosen grid pair is stable, $\lambda_{\min}$ will level off and remain strictly greater than a positive constant ($\lambda_{\min} \ge \beta^2 > 0$) even on very fine meshes, ensuring stability of the mixed finite element discretization.
    \item \textbf{Unstable Elements:} If the grid pair is unstable, $\lambda_{\min}$ will continuously drop toward zero as the mesh size decreases. This acts as a clear warning that the simulation will suffer from spurious pressure oscillations.
\end{itemize}


\subsection*{Mass Matrix \texorpdfstring{$\mathbf{M}_p$}{Mp}}
To check our results numerically we will have to construct the pressure mass matrix as well:

\begin{align*}
    \left(\mathbf{M}_p\right)_{ij} 
    = \int_{\Omega} \psi_j(x,y)\psi_i(x,y) \, d\Omega 
    &= \sum_T \int_{T} \psi_j(x,y)\psi_i(x,y) dxdy
    = \sum_T \int_{\hat{T}} \hat\psi_j(\xi_1,\xi_2)\hat\psi_i(\xi_1,\xi_2) \left|\mathbf{J}_T\right| \, d\xi_1d\xi_2\\[10pt]
    &= \sum_T \left|\mathbf{J}_T\right| \int_{\hat{T}} \hat\psi_j \hat\psi_i \, d\xi_1d\xi_2
\end{align*}

We can now turn to the local mass matrix:

\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{M}_p^{loc}
    &= \left|\mathbf{J}_T\right| \int_{\hat{T}} \hat\psi_j \hat\psi_i \, d\xi_1d\xi_2 
    = \left|\mathbf{J}_T\right| \int_{\hat{T}}
    \begin{bmatrix} 
    \displaystyle
        \hat{\psi}_1\hat{\psi}_1 & 
        \hat{\psi}_1\hat{\psi}_2 & 
        \hat{\psi}_1\hat{\psi}_3 \\ 
        \hat{\psi}_2\hat{\psi}_1 & 
        \hat{\psi}_2\hat{\psi}_2 & 
        \hat{\psi}_2\hat{\psi}_3 \\ 
        \hat{\psi}_3\hat{\psi}_1 & 
        \hat{\psi}_3\hat{\psi}_2 & 
        \hat{\psi}_3\hat{\psi}_3 
    \end{bmatrix}
    \, d\xi_1d\xi_2 
    = \left| 
    \begin{array}{l}
         \hat{\psi}_1 = 1 - \xi_1 - \xi_2 \\
         \hat{\psi}_2 = \xi_1 \\
         \hat{\psi}_3 = \xi_2
    \end{array} 
    \right| = \\[15pt]
    &= \left|\mathbf{J}_T\right| \int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1-\xi_1}      
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \displaystyle
        (1 - \xi_1 - \xi_2)(1 - \xi_1 - \xi_2) &
        \xi_1(1 - \xi_1 - \xi_2) &
        \xi_2(1 - \xi_1 - \xi_2) \\
        \xi_1(1 - \xi_1 - \xi_2) &
        \xi_1\xi_1 &
        \xi_2 \xi_1\\
        \xi_2(1 - \xi_1 - \xi_2) &
        \xi_2 \xi_1 &
        \xi_2\xi_2
    \end{bmatrix}
    \, d\xi_2d\xi_1 = \\[10pt]
    &= \frac{\left|\mathbf{J}_T\right|}{24}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        2 & 1 & 1\\
        1 & 2 & 1\\
        1 & 1 & 2
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{align*}

\subsection*{Numerical Verification of the Discrete Inf-Sup Condition}

We have already established that the $P_1$-iso-$P_1$ pairing is stable if and
only if the minimum eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min}$ of
\[
\hat{\mathbf{K}} = \mathbf{M}_p^{-1}\left(\mathbf{B}_x \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B}_x^T + \mathbf{B}_y \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B}_y^T\right)
\]
remains bounded away from zero as the mesh is refined. We verify this directly by solving the equivalent
generalized eigenvalue problem $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{p} = \lambda \mathbf{M}_p \mathbf{p}$, with $\mathbf{K} =
\mathbf{B}_x \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B}_x^T + \mathbf{B}_y \mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{B}_y^T$, over six uniform
refinement levels of a test domain. Since $\mathbf{A}$ is never needed in inverted form --- only
$\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_x^T$ and $\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_y^T$, i.e.\ $N_p$ right-hand sides with
$N_p \ll N_v$ --- $\mathbf{A}$ is factorized once via sparse LU and solved against these $N_p$ columns rather
than inverted densely.

The successive differences ($\Delta\lambda_{\min}$) decrease rapidly, indicating that the eigenvalue approaches a positive limiting value rather than converging to zero. This behavior provides clear, practical proof that our $P_1$-iso-$P_1$ setup is numerically stable and successfully satisfies the LBB condition.

In this section, we validate our $P_1\text{-iso-}P_1$ finite element solver using two distinct fluid engineering case studies: an \textit{Exchanger Device} and a \textit{Winding Pipe}. Both configurations operate under a creeping flow regime ($\mathrm{Re} \ll 1$), where viscous forces dominate.

The numerical results inside the \textit{Exchanger Device} reveal distinct kinematic adjustments driven by the variable cross-sectional area of the channel network. As observed in the velocity streamline distributions (Figure~\ref{fig:exchanger_streamlines}), the fluid velocity increases significantly at the turns, where the geometry narrows relative to the wider vertical passages. To preserve mass conservation under the incompressibility constraint $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$, the velocity field must accelerate through these local contractions, creating higher localized velocity gradients along the curved inner walls.

Furthermore, the simulation clearly shows fluid swirls (recirculation zones) inside the dead-end, cut-off extensions. Because Stokes flow has no inertia, the fluid balance simplifies to a strict equilibrium between pressure gradients and viscous friction:
\[
-\nu \Delta \mathbf{u} + \nabla p = \mathbf{0}
\]
As a result, the main stream takes the path of least resistance through the primary central channels. However, as this fast-moving core flow sweeps past the stagnant fluid trapped in the side cavities, it shears against it. This boundary shear transfers momentum across the open gap, dragging the passive fluid along and creating slow, circular vortices inside the extensions. The computed velocity field exhibits well-resolved stagnation regions and sharp shear interfaces, indicating stable and physically consistent behavior of the mixed finite element discretization.

To investigate the convergence behavior of the P1-iso-P1 discretization, a reference solution was first computed on the finest available mesh, obtained after seven uniform refinements of the initial domain geometry. Solutions on coarser meshes were then compared against this reference solution.

Since the discrete velocity and pressure spaces have different dimensions on different refinement levels, the solutions cannot be compared directly. Therefore, each coarse-grid solution was recursively transferred to the reference mesh using the finite-element embedding operator. For a solution computed on refinement level \(i\), the embedding matrices corresponding to all subsequent refinements were applied successively,
\[
\mathbf{u}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{emb}}
=
\mathbf{E}_{N}
\cdots
\mathbf{E}_{i+1}
\mathbf{E}_{i}
\mathbf{u}^{(i)},
\]
and analogously for the pressure field. After each embedding step, the mesh was refined and the next embedding matrix was constructed on the newly generated mesh. This recursive procedure produced velocity and pressure approximations living in the same finite-element spaces as the reference solution.

The errors were then defined by
\[
\mathbf{e}_{u_x}
=
\mathbf{u}_{x,\mathrm{emb}}
-
\mathbf{u}_{x,\mathrm{ref}},
\qquad
\mathbf{e}_{u_y}
=
\mathbf{u}_{y,\mathrm{emb}}
-
\mathbf{u}_{y,\mathrm{ref}},
\]
and
\[
\mathbf{e}_{p}
=
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{emb}}
-
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}.
\]

Their discrete \(L^2\)-norms were evaluated using the mass matrices corresponding to the fine and coarse spaces on the reference mesh,
\[
\|\mathbf{e}_u\|_{L^2(\Omega)}
=
\sqrt{
\mathbf{e}_{u_x}^{T}
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{fine}}
\mathbf{e}_{u_x}
+
\mathbf{e}_{u_y}^{T}
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{fine}}
\mathbf{e}_{u_y}
},
\]
and
\[
\|\mathbf{e}_p\|_{L^2(\Omega)}
=
\sqrt{
\mathbf{e}_{p}^{T}
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{coarse}}
\mathbf{e}_{p}
}.
\]

Finally, the experimental convergence rates were estimated from two consecutive refinement levels according to
\[
r
=
\frac{\log(E_h/E_{h/2})}{\log(2)},
\]
where \(E_h\) denotes the corresponding \(L^2\)-error.

\section{Introduction to Reduced-Order Modeling}\label{sec:rom_intro}
The finite element discretization developed in the preceding sections yields a numerically reliable, but computationally expensive, \emph{full-order model} (FOM): even at moderate refinement levels the $P_1$-iso-$P_1$ discretization produces velocity systems with $\mathcal{O}(10^5)$ degrees of freedom (Table~\ref{tab:stokes_convergence}), and every change in the physical configuration requires reassembling and resolving the full saddle-point system. This cost becomes prohibitive in \emph{many-query} settings --- parameter studies, optimization loops, or real-time control --- where the Stokes system must be solved repeatedly for many instances of a parameter vector $\boldsymbol\mu \in \mathcal{P} \subset \mathbb{R}^p$.
In the present study, $\boldsymbol\mu$ parametrizes a spatially varying kinematic viscosity field, and solving the FOM anew for every parameter instance is computationally wasteful: nearby parameters produce similar flow fields, so the resulting solution manifold, 
$$
\mathcal{M} = \left\{ (\mathbf{u}_h(\boldsymbol\mu), p_h) : \boldsymbol\mu \in \mathcal{P} \right\},
$$
possesses low intrinsic dimension despite living in a high-dimensional discrete space, and can be well approximated by a low-dimensional linear subspace. This observation is the central premise of \textit{reduced-order modeling} (ROM): rather than repeatedly solving the full-order system, one projects the problem onto a much smaller subspace and solves a reduced Galerkin system instead.

The construction of this reduced system naturally splits into two stages. In an \emph{offline} stage, performed once, a set of representative FOM solutions is precomputed and compressed into a low-dimensional reduced basis; any parameter-independent quantities needed to assemble the reduced operators are also built at this stage, regardless of computational cost, since it is paid only once. In the subsequent \emph{online} stage, a new parameter instance $\boldsymbol\mu$ is handled by assembling and solving only the small reduced system, at a cost independent of the underlying mesh resolution $N$. This offline--online decomposition is what ultimately makes ROM attractive for many-query settings: the expensive part of the computation is amortized once, while each additional query becomes essentially free by comparison.

\section{Parametrized Problem Formulation}
The \textit{exchanger device} consists of a pipe-like geometry in which the temperature, and consequently the
viscosity, varies along the flow direction. Rather than resolving this dependence by solving the coupled
advection--diffusion equation for the temperature field, the viscosity distribution is approximated directly
from values prescribed at \textit{five} representative locations inside the domain and reconstructed
throughout $\Omega$ by \textit{inverse-distance (Shepard) interpolation}. This parameterization is what makes
the problem amenable to reduced-order modeling.

\subsection{The Viscosity Parameter Vector \texorpdfstring{$\boldsymbol{\mu}$}{mu}}

To enable an efficient reduced-order formulation, the spatially varying kinematic viscosity is parameterized by a finite set of basis functions. Five normalized Shepard basis functions\(\{\psi_k(\mathbf{x})\}_{k=1}^{5}\) are constructed from prescribed locations distributed over the computational domain. The viscosity field is then represented as 

\[
\nu(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}) = \sum_{k=1}^{p}\mu_k\,\psi_{k}(\mathbf{x}) \ ,
\qquad
\psi_k(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{w_{k}(\mathbf{x})}{\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^p w_{j}(\mathbf{x})} \ ,
\qquad
w_{k}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{\max(d_{k}, \epsilon)^\gamma} \ ,
\qquad
d_{k} = \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_k\|_2 \ ,
\] 

where: 
\begin{itemize}    
    \item $\boldsymbol{\mu} =     
        \begin{bmatrix}        
            \mu_1 & \mu_2 & \mu_3 & \mu_4 & \mu_5    
        \end{bmatrix}^{T} \in \mathcal{P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{5}$ 
    is the vector of viscosity values assigned at the control points,     
    
    \item $p = 5$ is the number of control points distributed across the domain,     
    
    \item $\mathbf{x}_k \in \Omega$ is the fixed spatial position of the $k$-th control point,     
    
    \item $\gamma > 0$ is a power parameter controlling the decay of influence with distance.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Affine Decomposition of the Viscous Operator}

Since the viscosity field enters the weak form linearly, the corresponding viscous operator inherits an affine parameter dependence. Under this affine structure, the bilinear form decomposes into parameter-independent spatial terms scaled by the parameter coefficients $\mu_k$. Consequently, these spatial operators can be precomputed once and reused for every $\boldsymbol\mu \in \mathcal{P}$, forming the foundation of the reduced-order formulation.
Since the assembly treats the viscosity as piecewise constant on each element --- evaluated once at the
element's centroid $\mathbf{x}_T$, exactly as in the numerical assembly routine --- the affine structure of
$\nu(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol\mu)$ propagates directly to the global stiffness matrix:

\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol\mu)
    &= \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}_h} \nu(\mathbf{x}_T;\boldsymbol\mu)\, \mathbf{A}^{loc}_T
    = \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}_h} \left( \sum_{k=1}^{5}\mu_k\,\psi_{k}(\mathbf{x}_T) \right) \mathbf{A}^{loc}_T 
    = \sum_{k=1}^{5}\mu_k \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}_h} \psi_{k}(\mathbf{x}_T)\,\mathbf{A}^{loc}_T \\[8pt]
    &= \sum_{k=1}^{5}\mu_k\, \mathbf{A}_k,
\end{aligned}
\]

where $\mathbf{A}^{loc}_T$ denotes the element stiffness matrix scattered into the global index set (the same
local matrix as in Section~\ref{sec:stiffness_matrix}, but with the viscosity factor left out), and
\[
\mathbf{A}_k := \sum_{T \in \mathcal{T}_h} \psi_{k}(\mathbf{x}_T)\,\mathbf{A}^{loc}_T
\]
is entirely independent of $\boldsymbol\mu$. Each $\mathbf{A}_k$ is obtained by running the ordinary assembly
routine once with $\psi_k$ itself substituted in place of the viscosity field, so five parameter-independent
matrices $\mathbf{A}_1,\dots,\mathbf{A}_5$ are assembled and stored once, offline; online, $\mathbf{A}(\boldsymbol\mu)$
for any new parameter instance reduces to a five-term scalar-weighted sum.

\section{Data Generation}
In this section, we detail the data generation pipeline required to construct the reduced-order model and address the computational and mathematical caveats inherent to the process. 

\subsection{Full-Order Snapshot Collection}
To generate the training dataset of $n_s=100$ snapshots, we evaluate the high-fidelity model over a discrete parameter set $\Xi = \{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}, \dots, \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(n_s)}\} \subset \mathcal{P}$. To ensure a robust, space-filling exploration of the five-dimensional parameter domain, these instances were generated using \textit{Latin Hypercube Sampling} (LHS) over the uniform bounds $\mathcal{P} = [10, 200]^5$. 

\begin{wrapfigure}{l}{0.42\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{reduced-order_model/LHS_downprojected.png}
    \captionsetup{font=small}
    \caption{Down-projection of LHS parameter set $\Xi$ bounded by the convex hull of the parameter space $\mathcal{P} = [10, 200]^5$.}
    \label{fig:lhs_projection}
\end{wrapfigure}

To visually verify the spatial distribution of the sampling points across the 5D domain, the parameters were standardized and projected onto a two-dimensional subspace using \textit{principal component analysis} (PCA). Figure~\ref{fig:lhs_projection} displays the down-projected set $\Xi$ alongside the convex hull of the parameter space $\mathcal{P}$. While the projected samples visually cluster toward the center of the subspace rather than filling the entire boundary uniformly, this is a consequence of dimensionality reduction. By the Central Limit Theorem, linear projections of high-dimensional uniform variables naturally converge toward a normal distribution. The underlying LHS scheme successfully achieves excellent space-filling properties, effectively capturing the global parameter behavior across the full high-dimensional domain.

\vspace{7pt}
Next, by exploiting the affine decomposition of the viscous operator,

\[
A(\boldsymbol{\mu}) = \sum_{k=1}^5 \mu_k A_k,
\]

we eliminate the computationally prohibitive requirement of generating and storing $n_s$ dense spatial viscosity fields, and instead assemble the global stiffness matrix for any configuration via a scalar combination of the five precomputed operators $A_k$.

The full-order saddle-point system was then solved for each $\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(i)} \in \Xi$ utilizing a \textit{parallelized computational framework} distributed across 10 CPU cores to obtain 
$\{ (\mathbf{u}_h(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)}), p_h(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(1)})), \dots, (\mathbf{u}_h(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(n_s)}), p_h(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{(n_s)})) \}$. Aggregating these discrete solutions produces the global snapshot matrices $\mathbf{S}_{u_x}, \mathbf{S}_{u_y} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_v \times n_s}$ and $\mathbf{S}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{N_p \times n_s}$. To be more specific:

\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{S}_{u_x} &= \left[ \mathbf{u}_x^{(1)} \Big\vert \dots \Big\vert \mathbf{u}_x^{(100)} \right] \in \mathbb{R}^{96\,817 \times 100} \\
    \mathbf{S}_{u_y} &= \left[ \mathbf{u}_y^{(1)} \Big\vert \dots \Big\vert \mathbf{u}_y^{(100)} \right] \in \mathbb{R}^{96\,817 \times 100} \\
    \mathbf{S}_{p} \ &= \left[ \mathbf{p}^{(1)} \Big\vert \dots \Big\vert \mathbf{p}^{(100)} \right] \in \mathbb{R}^{24\,473 \times 100}
\end{aligned}
\]

The total velocity snapshot matrix $\mathbf{S}_u$ is also introduced to make the computations easier later:

\[
\mathbf{S}_u = \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{S}_{u_x} \\[7pt]
    \mathbf{S}_{u_y}
\end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_v \times n_s}, \quad 
\mathbf{S}_u = \left[ \mathbf{u}^{(1)} \Big\vert \dots \Big\vert \mathbf{u}^{(100)} \right] \in \mathbb{R}^{193\,634 \times 100}
\]

\subsection{Supremizer Enrichment for Inf-Sup Stability}

Although the full-order $P_1$-iso-$P_1$ discretization satisfies the discrete inf-sup condition, this property is not automatically inherited by independently constructed reduced velocity and pressure spaces. A reduced basis obtained solely by applying \textit{proper orthogonal decomposition} (POD) separately to the velocity and pressure snapshots may violate the discrete LBB condition at the reduced level, producing an \textit{unstable reduced-order model} even though the full-order spaces it was built from are themselves stable.

Stability is recovered by enriching the reduced velocity space with \textit{supremizer modes}, which are derived as follows.

For a given pressure field $q \in Q$, the velocity supremizer $t \in V$ maximizes the discrete velocity-pressure divergence coupling $\int_\Omega q \, (\nabla \cdot v) \, d\Omega$. By the Riesz representation theorem, $t$ uniquely satisfies the variational problem:

\begin{tcolorbox}[
    colframe=ForestGreen, 
    colback=ForestGreen!7!white, 
    sharp corners
]
\begin{center}
    $\displaystyle\int_{\Omega} \nabla t : \nabla v \, d\Omega = \int_\Omega q \, (\nabla \cdot v) \, d\Omega \qquad \forall v \in V$
\end{center}
\end{tcolorbox}

$t$, test functions $v$, and pressure $q$ can be expanded in terms of their finite element basis functions and later substituted back into the variational problem: 
\[
t = \sum_{j=1}^{2N_v} \mathbf{t}_j \boldsymbol{\phi}_j, \qquad 
v = \sum_{i=1}^{2N_v} \mathbf{v}_i \boldsymbol{\phi}_i, \qquad 
q = \sum_{k=1}^{N_p} \mathbf{p}_k \boldsymbol{\psi}_k, \qquad
\]

\[
\begin{aligned}    
\sum_{i=1}^{2N_v} \sum_{j=1}^{2N_v} \mathbf{v}_i \left( \int_\Omega \nabla \boldsymbol{\phi}_i : \nabla \boldsymbol{\phi}_j \, d\Omega \right) \mathbf{t}_j 
&= \sum_{i=1}^{2N_v} \sum_{k=1}^{N_p} \mathbf{v}_i \left( \int_\Omega \boldsymbol{\psi}_k (\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\phi}_i) \, d\Omega \right) \mathbf{p}_k \\[15pt]
\mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{X}_u \mathbf{t} 
&= \mathbf{v}^T \mathbf{B}_h^T \mathbf{p}
\end{aligned}
\]

Since this relation holds for arbitrary $\mathbf{v}$, we obtain the discrete system
\[
\mathbf{X}_u \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{B}_h^T \mathbf{p} \implies \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{X}_u^{-1} \mathbf{B}_h^T \mathbf{p}.
\]

Now, for every pressure snapshot $\mathbf{p}_h(\boldsymbol\mu^{(k)})$, $k=1,\dots,n_s$, the corresponding algebraic supremizer
solution $\mathbf{t}^{(k)}$ is obtained by solving the auxiliary linear system

\[
\mathbf{X}_u\, \mathbf{t}^{(k)} = \mathbf{B}_h^T\, \mathbf{p}_h(\boldsymbol\mu^{(k)}), \, \qquad \text{where:} \quad
\mathbf{B}_h^T = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{B}_{xD}^T \\[7pt]
    \mathbf{B}_{yD}^T
\end{bmatrix}, \quad
\mathbf{X}_u = \begin{bmatrix}
    \mathbf{A}_{D} & \mathbf{0} \\[7pt]
    \mathbf{0}     & \mathbf{A}_{D}
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

\vspace{1em}
By first constructing the global saddle-point matrix $\mathbf{K}$ with unit viscosity ($\nu = 1$) and enforcing homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, the required operators are extracted directly via matrix slicing. Slicing rows $[0, 2N_v)$ from $\mathbf{K}$ yields a rectangular matrix with the corresponding needed operators: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item the velocity block operator $\mathbf{X}_u = \mathbf{K}[0:2N_v, \, 0:2N_v] \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_v\times 2N_v}$,
    \item the discrete gradient block $\mathbf{B}_h^T = \mathbf{K}[0:2N_v, \, 2N_v:2N_v+N_p] \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_v\times N_p}$.
\end{itemize}
Because both matrices are extracted from the exact same Dirichlet-corrected rows of $\mathbf{K}$, they naturally inherit identical boundary treatments. This approach directly reuses the existing Stokes assembly pipeline while keeping the underlying computations explicit and transparent.

Critically, $\mathbf{X}_u$ is assembled only once at $\nu = 1$, rather than at the viscosity described by parameter vector $\boldsymbol\mu^{(k)}$ of each snapshot. Since the discrete gradient matrix $\mathbf{B}_h^T$ depends solely on geometry and carries no viscosity dependence, computing the supremizer solutions $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{X}_u^{-1}\mathbf{B}_h^T\mathbf{p}$ involves a single, parameter-independent matrix inverse $\mathbf{X}_u^{-1}$, applicable unchanged to every pressure snapshot regardless of which $\boldsymbol\mu^{(k)}$ produced it.

Collecting these linear solves over the full snapshot set yields the multi-column global system:

\[
\mathbf{X}_u \mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{B}_h^{T}\mathbf{S}_p.
\]

In practice, rather than computing an explicit inverse, $\mathbf{X}_u$ is converted to CSC format and its sparse LU factorization is computed once. The complete multi-column right-hand side matrix $\mathbf{B}_h^{T}\mathbf{S}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_v\times n_s}$ is then assembled in a single step, allowing the entire matrix of supremizer snapshots $\mathbf{S}_t$ to be resolved via back-substitution in one pass:

\[
\mathbf{S}_t = \mathbf{X}_u^{-1} \left(\mathbf{B}_h^{T}\mathbf{S}_p\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_v\times n_s},
\qquad
\mathbf{S}_t = \left[\,\mathbf{t}^{(1)} \,\middle|\, \cdots \,\middle|\, \mathbf{t}^{(n_s)}\,\right] \in \mathbb{R}^{193\,634 \times 100}
\]

Reusing the pre-factored LU operator across all $n_s$ snapshot columns simultaneously drastically reduces the computational cost compared to solving snapshot vectors sequentially or refactorizing per snapshot.

\subsection{Velocity Snapshot Homogenization via Boundary Lifting}
To ensure that the reduced velocity basis functions belong to the homogeneous space $X$ with $\mathbf{u}|_{\Gamma_D} = \mathbf{0}$, the inhomogeneous boundary data must be removed from the raw full-order velocity snapshots prior to POD.

Following the weak formulation decomposition introduced above (\ref{eq:stokes_weak}), every total physical velocity field snapshot $\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)})$ is represented as:
\[
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}) = \mathbf{u}_h(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\mu}^{(k)}) + \mathbf{r}_g(\mathbf{x}),
\]
where $\mathbf{r}_g \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_v}$ is the discrete parameter-independent lifting vector defined earlier (\ref{eq:lifting_function}):

Given the raw velocity snapshot matrix $\mathbf{S}_{u}$, the homogenized snapshot matrix $\mathbf{S}_{u_h} \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_v \times n_v}$ is obtained by subtracting the lifting vector column-wise across all $n_s$ snapshots:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{S}_{u_h} = \mathbf{S}_{u} - \mathbf{r}_g \boldsymbol{1}_{n_s}^T \in \mathbb{R}^{2N_v \times n_s},
\label{eq:velocity_homogenization}
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol{1}_{n_s} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_s}$ is a vector of ones. Every column vector $\mathbf{u}_h^{(k)}$ in $\mathbf{S}_{u_h}$ strictly satisfies homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions ($\mathbf{u}_h^{(k)} \in X$), ensuring that all POD basis modes extracted from $\mathbf{S}_u$ naturally inherit homogeneous boundary values by construction.

\subsubsection*{First Einsum}
\label{sub:first_example}

\begin{lstlisting}[
    language=Python, 
    frame=single,                  
    backgroundcolor=\color{white}, 
    basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize, 
    breaklines=true                 
    Overfull \hbox
]
A_local = np.zeros(shape=(Nt, 3, 3))
for i in range(3):
    for j in range(i, 3):

        grad_i = test_function_derivatives[i]
        grad_j = test_function_derivatives[j]

        val = np.einsum('i,nij,j->n', 
                        grad_j, Q_mat, grad_i)

        val *= kinematic_viscosity / (2 * det_J)

        A_local[:, i, j] = val
        A_local[:, j, i] = val
\end{lstlisting}

\[
\begin{array}{ccc}
    \left[\psi_1, \psi_2\right]  
    & 
    \left[
    \begin{array}{cc}
        q^n_{11} & q^n_{12} \\
        q^n_{21} & q^n_{22}
    \end{array}
    \right]
    & \left[\psi_1, \psi_2\right] \\
    \psi_i & q^n_{ij} & \psi_j
\end{array}
\]

\[
\sum_{i=2}^2\sum_{j=2}^2\psi_i q^n_{ij} \psi_j = A^n \in \mathbb{R}^n
\]

\subsubsection*{Second Einsum}
\label{sub:Second_example}

\begin{lstlisting}[
    language=Python, 
    frame=single,                  % Draws the box around the code
    backgroundcolor=\color{white}, % Background color inside the box
    basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize, % Makes the font very small
    breaklines=true                 % Automatically wraps long lines to fix Overfull \hbox
]
A_local = np.einsum('mi,txy,nj->tmn', 
                    test_function_derivatives, Q_mat, test_function_derivatives)
A_local *= (kinematic_viscosity / (2.0 * det_J))[:, None, None]
\end{lstlisting}

\[
\begin{array}{ccccc}
    
    \left[
    \begin{array}{c}
         \left[\psi_1^1,\psi_2^1\right]\\[3pt]
         \left[\psi_1^2,\psi_2^2\right]\\[3pt]
         \left[\psi_1^3,\psi_2^3\right]     
    \end{array}
    \right]
    &
    \left[
    \begin{array}{cc}
        q^t_{11} & q^t_{12} \\
        q^t_{21} & q^t_{22}
    \end{array}
    \right]
    &
    \left[
    \begin{array}{c}
         \left[\psi_1^1,\psi_2^1\right]\\[3pt]
         \left[\psi_1^2,\psi_2^2\right]\\[3pt]
         \left[\psi_1^3,\psi_2^3\right]     
    \end{array}
    \right]
    &
    \longrightarrow
    &
    \left[
    \begin{array}{ccc}
        A^t_{11} & A^t_{12} & A^t_{13}\\
        A^t_{21} & A^t_{22} & A^t_{23}\\
        A^t_{31} & A^t_{32} & A^t_{33}
    \end{array}
    \right]\\[2em]
    \psi^m_i & q^t_{xy} & \psi^n_j &  & A^t_{mn}
\end{array}
\]
So by using indexation and sums we notice that the structure has not really changed since the first example~\ref{sub:first_example}:

\[
\sum_{i=1}^2\sum_{j=1}^2\psi^m_iq^t_{ij}\psi^n_j = A^t_{mn}\in\mathbb{R}^{t \times m\times n}
\]
